## Appendix A — The inventory
### Twenty statements, four patterns

A longer read on where the weight has gone out of your life. Rate each from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Total each section. Your highest section is where to aim the Protocol first.

**A. Self-betrayal**
- I make promises to myself that I break within days.
- I've stopped believing my own resolutions while I'm making them.
- I'd be embarrassed if someone saw the gap between what I say and what I do.
- I can't remember the last promise to myself I kept all the way through.
- I trust other people's word more than my own.
Section total: ___ / 25

**B. Unenforced standards**
- I have standards I state out loud and don't enforce.
- When I violate a standard, nothing happens.
- I treat my own rules as suggestions.
- I've lowered a standard rather than meet it.
- I couldn't name a cost I've paid this month for breaking a rule I set.
Section total: ___ / 25

**C. Shielding**
- Someone usually absorbs the fallout of my choices before it reaches me.
- I avoid looking at the numbers (money, weight, hours) that would tell me the truth.
- I find a reason why this time was different.
- I'd rather feel bad about a failure than pay for it.
- I explain my way out of consequences more than I face them.
Section total: ___ / 25

**D. Drift**
- Life feels flat, even when nothing's wrong.
- I chase stimulation (screens, purchases, drinks, arguments) to feel something.
- I can't say why I'm unmotivated.
- I blame circumstances in situations I know were my call.
- I've lost respect for myself and can't name the day it started.
Section total: ___ / 25

**Reading it:** 5–10, light. 11–17, moderate, and it's eroding something quietly. 18–25, this is a primary pattern, and the man you intend to be is a stranger to the one you are.

## Appendix B — The research behind it
### So you know it isn't just my opinion

Everything in this book I learned the hard way first. But I'm not asking you to take my word for it, and you shouldn't. Here's where the claims come from.

**That behavior is shaped by what follows it.** B. F. Skinner's operant conditioning, laid out in *The Behavior of Organisms* (1938) and *Science and Human Behavior* (1953), remains the foundation of behavioral science. Reinforcement, punishment, and the drift of behavior that produces no consequence are his.

**That the brain learns from prediction error.** Wolfram Schultz and colleagues showed in 1997 that dopamine neurons fire in response to the difference between expected and actual reward, which is the signal the brain uses to update its model. No outcome, no error, no update.

**That now beats later by a wide margin.** George Ainslie described delay discounting in 1975: the systematic preference for a smaller reward now over a larger one later. The literature since is enormous and consistent. Bringing a consequence into the present is the practical answer to it.

**That self-trust is built from experience.** Albert Bandura introduced self-efficacy in 1977 and spent decades showing that mastery experiences, doing the thing and seeing it work, are its strongest source. Verbal persuasion is its weakest.

**That votes have to be counted.** James Clear's *Atomic Habits* (2018) is the source for identity as the accumulation of behavioral evidence.

**That severed consequence produces passivity.** Martin Seligman and Steven Maier's learned helplessness experiments (1967) showed what happens when an organism learns its actions don't affect outcomes. Seligman's later work on the human version is in *Learned Optimism* (1990).

**That you have to bind your future self.** Jon Elster's *Ulysses and the Sirens* (1979) is the philosophical treatment of precommitment. Dan Ariely and Klaus Wertenbroch showed in 2002 that people who set binding deadlines for themselves perform better than those who don't. Nassim Taleb's *Skin in the Game* (2018) makes the broader argument that nobody learns anything without exposure to the downside.

**That accountability changes behavior.** The research on commitment devices and social accountability, summarized in Katherine Milkman's *How to Change* (2021), consistently finds that standards known to another person are kept more often.

**On Scripture.** Galatians 6:7–8; Proverbs 10:4, 12:11, 12:15; Hebrews 12:5–11; Luke 15:11–32; Matthew 5:37; Romans 6:23; 2 Corinthians 5:10.

**On Marcus Aurelius.** *Meditations* 10.16, in Gregory Hays's translation (2002).
