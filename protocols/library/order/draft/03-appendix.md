## Appendix A — The inventory
### Twenty statements, four patterns

A longer read on where your order breaks. Rate each from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Total each section. Your highest section is where to aim the build first.

**A. Decision load**
- I'm tired by daily decisions that shouldn't be hard.
- I spend real energy deciding what to eat, wear, or do next.
- My week has to be planned from scratch rather than following a pattern.
- I second-guess decisions after I've made them.
- By evening I'm making choices I'd never make in the morning.
Section total: ___ / 25

**B. Willpower reliance**
- I have to force myself to do basic things.
- I need to feel motivated before I'll start.
- My good habits last a couple of weeks and then collapse.
- I keep restarting the same routine.
- I think of myself as undisciplined.
Section total: ___ / 25

**C. Environment**
- My phone is the first thing I touch in the morning and the last at night.
- My desk, truck, or garage is cluttered and I've stopped noticing.
- The things I want to do are harder to start than the things I don't.
- Junk food, screens, and distractions are within reach all day.
- I've never deliberately designed a room to support a habit.
Section total: ___ / 25

**D. Collapse under stress**
- When pressure goes up, my routines are the first thing to go.
- A bad week undoes a good month.
- Travel or sickness resets me to zero.
- I don't have a minimum set of structures I protect no matter what.
- I've had at least one season where the basics (sleep, food, exercise) failed completely.
Section total: ___ / 25

**Reading it:** 5–10, light. 11–17, moderate, and it shows up the first bad week. 18–25, this is a primary pattern, and the next hard season will find it unless you build first.

## Appendix B — The research behind it
### So you know it isn't just my opinion

Everything in this book I learned the hard way first. But I'm not asking you to take my word for it, and you shouldn't. Here's where the claims come from.

**That decision quality drops as decisions accumulate.** Shai Danziger and colleagues published the parole-board study in 2011. A rebuttal the same year argued that case scheduling, not fatigue, could explain the pattern, and the debate continues. I've kept the idea because the underlying claim, that the prefrontal cortex is metabolically expensive and tires with use, is well established, and because Roy Baumeister's broader work on self-regulation as a depletable resource, whatever its exact size in a lab, matches what every man sees in himself at nine at night.

**That some mental load is just bad design.** John Sweller introduced cognitive load theory in 1988, distinguishing the load that's intrinsic to a task, the load that's worth paying to learn, and the extraneous load imposed by poor design. The Order Protocol is aimed at the third.

**That more options cost more than they give.** Barry Schwartz's *The Paradox of Choice* (2004) gathers the research on choice overload: more options produce more anxiety, less satisfaction, more paralysis, and more regret. Sheena Iyengar's jam study (2000) is the one everyone cites.

**That habits run on a different system than decisions.** Charles Duhigg's *The Power of Habit* (2012) popularized the cue-routine-reward loop. Wendy Wood's *Good Habits, Bad Habits* (2019) is the researcher's version: roughly forty percent of daily behavior is habitual, and habitual behavior runs through the basal ganglia rather than the prefrontal cortex. James Clear's *Atomic Habits* (2018) is where "make it obvious, easy, attractive, satisfying" comes from; Part IV's four moves are a reworking of his.

**That environment beats intention.** Richard Thaler and Cass Sunstein's *Nudge* (2008) laid out the case that defaults and environment shape behavior more reliably than persuasion. Brigitte Madrian and Dennis Shea's 2001 study of automatic 401(k) enrollment is the source for the jump in participation when the default flips.

**That the field reveals the man.** Jordan Peterson's sixth rule, "Set your house in perfect order before you criticize the world," makes the same argument Proverbs 24 makes, and *12 Rules for Life* (2018) is where most men encountered it.

**On Scripture.** Genesis 1:1–2:3 and 1:28; Proverbs 14:1 and 24:30–34; Luke 9:10–17; Exodus 20:8–11; Colossians 1:17; Hebrews 3:4.

**On Marcus Aurelius.** *Meditations* 2.5, in Gregory Hays's translation (2002).
