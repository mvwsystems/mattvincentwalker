# The Boundary Protocol
## Where you end — and what to do about everything you've been carrying that isn't yours

---

## Before you start

For nearly twenty years I was the man people called.

That's what a pastor is, if he's any good at it. The phone rings at two in the morning and you answer. Somebody's son is in a cell, somebody's wife has left, somebody's father is dying in a room that smells like bleach, and you go. I was good at it. I was proud of being good at it. If you'd asked me what it cost, I'd have told you it was the job, and the job was a calling, and a calling doesn't send invoices.

Here's what I didn't know. I wasn't carrying those people. I was carrying the feeling that I had to. There's a difference, and it took me losing almost everything to see it.

When my own year came, when the income went and the drinking started and my marriage was hanging by a thread, I found out I had no idea how to let anyone carry me. I had built my whole identity on being needed, and a man who is needed cannot afford to need. So I didn't. I went down alone, in a house full of people who loved me, because I had never learned where I ended and they began.

Afterward I went and studied it. I trained at the Townsend Institute, the school built on the work of Henry Cloud and John Townsend. Their book *Boundaries* has sold millions of copies, and I think I know why. Nobody teaches men this. We're taught to carry. Nobody tells us what's ours to carry and what isn't, and so we pick up everything, and then we wonder why we're tired in a way sleep doesn't fix.

That's what this is about. Not how to be less kind. How to find the line.

### What a boundary is

A boundary is a property line. It marks what's yours and what isn't. Inside the line: your thoughts, your feelings, your choices, your time, your body, your money, your consequences. Outside the line: everyone else's.

That's it. It isn't a wall. It isn't an ultimatum. It isn't a way of punishing someone. It's a line in the dirt that says *this is mine to tend, and that is yours.*

Men without boundaries do two things, usually at the same time. They take on what isn't theirs: other people's moods, other people's problems, other people's consequences. And they give away what is: their time, their evenings, their right to want anything. They call this love. They call it leadership, or loyalty, or service.

It is none of those things. It's a failure to know where you end. And it will take everything you have, because it has no bottom.

### What this is, and isn't

This isn't a book about becoming harder. If you're reading it, you're probably already carrying a quiet contempt for the men who seem to have no trouble saying no, and I'm not asking you to become one of them. The men with walls are just as lost as the men with none. They've solved the problem by leaving the room.

This is a protocol. A short, honest way to find out what you're actually carrying (Part I), understand why trying harder has never once worked (Parts II and III), see the line clearly (Part IV), draw it (Part V), say it out loud (Part VI), and hold it over years (Part VII).

I'm a Christian, and Part III says so plainly. If you've been told that a man who follows Jesus has no business setting limits, I want you to read what Jesus actually did. He walked away from crowds. He let people leave. He told Peter no to his face. If you're not where I am on Him, read it anyway. The diagnosis in Part I has been true for every exhausted, resentful, good man I've ever sat across from, and the five moves in Part V work regardless of what you do with Part III.

The research is in the back, so you know it isn't just my opinion. Family systems theory, trauma research, the neuroscience of why "no" hurts. It's there when you want it.

One more thing. Don't skim this. The men who need it most are the ones who'll read it fast so they can get back to carrying something. Give it an hour. Bring a pen.

## Part I — The Diagnosis
### The weight that isn't yours

You won't find your boundary problem by asking whether you have one. You'll say no. Every man does. You'll say you're just a hard worker, a good son, a loyal friend, the guy who shows up.

You find it by asking a different question: *Who am I quietly angry at, and what did I give them that I didn't want to give?*

That's not bitterness. That's a map.

### The symptoms

Check the ones that are true in the last ninety days. Not the ones you'd admit to. The ones that are true.

☐ I feel guilty when I say no, even when no is obviously right
☐ I'm doing for other people what they should be doing for themselves
☐ I resent someone I'm helping
☐ When someone close to me is upset, I feel like it's my job to fix it
☐ I'm not sure what I actually want anymore
☐ I avoid conflict by agreeing, then do what I wanted anyway, or do nothing
☐ I'm tired in a way that a weekend doesn't touch
☐ People take me for granted, and I can't say I've ever told them not to

Three or more is a boundary problem. Six or more is a boundary collapse, and you're probably the last one to know, because everyone around you benefits from it.

These aren't signs that you love too much. They're signs that you've lost the line, and nobody is going to give it back to you. You have to draw it.

### Resentment is the alarm

Here is the single most useful thing I know about this subject. Resentment is never random. It is the precise feeling of having crossed your own line, and it points straight at the place you crossed it.

When you feel resentment toward someone you're helping, you have given past the point of real generosity. You've moved from *I want to* into *I have to*, and your soul is protesting. The resentment isn't evidence that you're unloving. It's evidence that you abandoned yourself to manage someone else.

Most men treat resentment as something to be ashamed of, so they bury it, and then it leaks out sideways. Sarcasm. Coldness. A short temper with the kids over nothing. A drink. The thing you buried at the office comes home and bites your family.

Don't bury it. Read it. Resentment is the fee you pay for crossing your own boundaries. The cure isn't more giving. It's a better line.

### How you learned it

Nobody is born without boundaries. They're taken from you, usually early, usually by people who meant well.

A father who used guilt as a steering wheel. A mother whose moods were the weather in the house, and you learned to read the sky before you learned to read. A home where having a need was treated as a betrayal, or where you were the one holding the adults together. A church that taught you that wanting anything for yourself was the first step toward hell.

You learned that love meant absorption. That saying no was leaving. That the way to be safe was to be necessary. And it worked. It got you through. Then you grew up and kept doing it, and now it's eating you.

I'm not going to spend long here, because I don't think you need to understand your childhood in order to draw a line today. But you need to know this much: the thing that feels like your character is actually a strategy. Strategies can be replaced.

### Exercise 1 — The weight inventory

Write down everything you're carrying right now. All of it. The project at work nobody else will touch. Your brother's finances. Your mother's loneliness. Your wife's mood. The friend who calls every week with the same problem and never takes the advice. The church thing you said yes to in a weak moment three years ago.

Ten items minimum. Then next to each one, write a single word: *mine*, *theirs*, or *ours*.

1. ________________________________________  mine / theirs / ours
2. ________________________________________  mine / theirs / ours
3. ________________________________________  mine / theirs / ours
4. ________________________________________  mine / theirs / ours
5. ________________________________________  mine / theirs / ours
6. ________________________________________  mine / theirs / ours
7. ________________________________________  mine / theirs / ours
8. ________________________________________  mine / theirs / ours
9. ________________________________________  mine / theirs / ours
10. ________________________________________  mine / theirs / ours

Be honest about *ours*. Most men use it as a hiding place. If you're carrying ninety percent of it, it's not ours.

Now count the *theirs*. That number is the weight you're carrying that was never yours. It's also, roughly, the amount of your life you'll get back when you put it down.

### Exercise 2 — The resentment map

Three people you resent. Not hate. Resent: the low, steady, tired kind. For each one, answer three questions in writing.

1. What have I been giving this person that I didn't want to give?
2. What was I afraid would happen if I stopped?
3. Has it happened to anyone else who stopped? What actually happened to them?

The third question is the one that matters. Almost always, the catastrophe you're preventing has happened to someone else, and they survived it, and in many cases they grew up because of it.

### The five fears

Behind every boundary that doesn't get drawn, there's a fear. Mark the one that's yours. You can mark two. Don't mark five.

☐ **Rejection.** If I set a limit, they'll leave.
☐ **Conflict.** If I say no, there'll be a fight, and I can't take the fight.
☐ **Guilt.** If something happens to them, I won't be able to live with myself.
☐ **Being seen as selfish.** What will people think of me?
☐ **Losing myself.** If I'm not the one they need, I don't know who I am.

Mine was the last one. If you've read *The Grounding Protocol*, you know being needed was one of the two false grounds I stood on for twenty years. A man who stands on being needed cannot set a boundary, because every boundary is a small step toward not being needed, and that feels like a small death.

It isn't. It's the opposite. But you don't find that out by thinking about it. You find it out by drawing one line and surviving.

Write it, in your own hand:

**I have been carrying ______________________ because I was afraid of ______________________.**

Don't fix it yet. Part II is about why you've never been able to simply decide to stop, and why that isn't your fault, and why it's still your responsibility.
