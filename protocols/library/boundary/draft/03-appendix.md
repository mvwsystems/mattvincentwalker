## Appendix A — The inventory
### Twenty statements, four patterns

A longer read on the four ways the line gets lost. Rate each from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Total each section. Your highest section is where to aim the Protocol first.

**A. Carrying**
- I feel responsible for how the people close to me are feeling.
- When someone has a problem, I start solving it before they've asked.
- I'm tired in a way rest doesn't fix.
- I've taken on a job, task, or role that someone else should be doing.
- I feel anxious when I'm not needed.
Section total: ___ / 25

**B. Pleasing**
- I say yes and then regret it.
- I change how I act depending on who's in the room.
- I avoid conflict even when I know I'm right.
- I replay conversations looking for signs someone's upset with me.
- I over-explain my decisions so people won't be mad.
Section total: ___ / 25

**C. Rescuing**
- I've covered for someone's choices so they wouldn't face the fallout.
- I've lent money, time, or help I didn't want to give.
- I'd rather fix it myself than watch someone struggle.
- I feel guilty when I let someone experience a consequence.
- I have at least one relationship where I'm doing most of the work.
Section total: ___ / 25

**D. Self-governance**
- I act on impulses I'd planned not to act on. Food, money, screens, words.
- I stay in a bad mood longer than I mean to.
- I let my feelings in the moment override commitments I've made.
- I say no to other people more easily than I say no to myself.
- I ruminate on a problem long after thinking has stopped helping.
Section total: ___ / 25

**Reading it:** 5–10, light. 11–17, moderate, and it shows up under pressure. 18–25, this is a primary pattern, and it's probably the one the people around you could have named for you.

### The weight log

For seven to fourteen days, every time you notice resentment, the urge to rescue, or a yes you didn't mean, write it down. The pattern will tell you what the inventory can't.

Date / time ___________  Who _________________________________________
What they wanted, or what I jumped in to do ___________________________
What I felt in my body _______________________________________________
What I was afraid would happen if I didn't ____________________________
What I did __________________________________________________________
Mine / theirs / ours

## Appendix B — The research behind it
### So you know it isn't just my opinion

Everything in this book I learned the hard way first. But I'm not asking you to take my word for it, and you shouldn't. Here's where the claims come from.

**That boundaries are property lines, and that love requires them.** The frame for this whole book comes from Henry Cloud and John Townsend, whose *Boundaries* (1992) is the book that put the word into the language. Their distinction between being responsible *to* and responsible *for*, and the burdens-and-loads reading of Galatians 6, is theirs. I trained under their institute. Cloud's *Necessary Endings* covers the hard rooms in Part VII.

**That "no" registers as pain.** Naomi Eisenberger's lab at UCLA showed in 2003, and in two decades of follow-up, that social rejection activates the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex, the same region that processes the distress of physical pain. Matthew Lieberman's *Social* lays the evidence out for a general reader. This is why saying no "feels like it'll kill you." Your brain is treating it that way.

**That appeasement is a trauma response.** Pete Walker, in *Complex PTSD: From Surviving to Thriving* (2013), named the fawn response as a fourth survival strategy alongside fight, flight, and freeze. His description of the fawner, hypervigilant to others' moods and absent to his own, is the man in Part II.

**That staying yourself under pressure is a skill, and it has a name.** Murray Bowen's family systems theory, laid out in *Family Therapy in Clinical Practice* (1978), built everything around differentiation of self. Edwin Friedman carried it into leadership in *A Failure of Nerve* (2007), arguing that the differentiated leader, not the most empathetic one, is the one who keeps a family or an organization healthy. Fusion and cutoff as twin failures is Bowen's.

**That the muscle underneath can be built.** Distress tolerance as a trainable capacity is central to Marsha Linehan's dialectical behavior therapy (1993). The principle that tolerance grows under graded load, not avoidance, runs through the exposure literature going back to Joseph Wolpe in the 1950s.

**That how you learned to attach shapes how you hold a line.** John Bowlby's attachment theory (1969 onward) and Mary Ainsworth's "strange situation" studies established the secure, anxious, and avoidant patterns. The adult-relationship application that Part II leans on is in Amir Levine and Rachel Heller's *Attached* (2010).

**That you have to believe your choices matter.** Julian Rotter introduced locus of control in 1966. Decades of research since tie an internal locus to the willingness to act on one's own behalf, which is what a boundary is.

**That rescuing keeps people stuck.** Melody Beattie's *Codependent No More* (1986) is the book that named the pattern for a general audience. The clinical literature on enabling in addiction treatment makes the same case: shielding a person from consequences predicts continued use.

**On burdens and loads.** The two Greek words in Galatians 6:2 and 6:5 are *baros* and *phortion*. The distinction is standard in the lexicons (Bauer-Danker-Arndt-Gingrich) and in most commentaries on Galatians. It's not a trick of translation. Paul chose different words.

**On Jesus.** Everything in Part III about what He did is in the text: Mark 1:35, Mark 3:31–35, Luke 10:38–42, Matthew 16:23, John 6:66–67, Mark 10:17–22, Luke 15:11–32.

**On Marcus Aurelius.** Both lines are from the *Meditations*, in Gregory Hays's translation (2002).
