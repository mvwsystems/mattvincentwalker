## Part IV — Where You End
### The property lines

Before you can draw a line, you have to be able to see it. Most men can't. They've lived so long without one that the whole field looks like theirs, or none of it does.

So here it is, as plainly as I can put it.

### What's yours

- Your thoughts
- Your feelings
- Your choices
- Your values
- Your time
- Your body
- Your money and your things
- Your consequences

### What isn't

- Their thoughts
- Their feelings
- Their choices
- Their values
- Their time
- Their bodies
- Their money and their things
- Their consequences

That's the whole map. Everything in the first list is yours to tend, and nobody gets to take it without your consent. Everything in the second is theirs, and you don't get to take it either, no matter how much you love them or how obviously they're about to drive into a wall.

### Responsible to, not for

If you take one sentence out of this book, take this one. **You are responsible to people. You are not responsible for them.**

Responsible *to* means: I owe you love. I owe you the truth. I owe you faithfulness, and the help I've promised, and my presence when I've said I'll be there.

Responsible *for* means: your feelings are my job. Your choices are my job. Your happiness, your growth, your outcomes, your sobriety, your mood on Tuesday. All mine.

The second one sounds like love and it's a prison for both of you. It makes you a manager and the other person a dependent. Nobody grows. Everybody's tired.

### What a boundary isn't

Men who are new to this get it wrong in predictable ways, so let me save you some time.

A boundary isn't a wall. A wall keeps everything out. A boundary lets love in and keeps responsibility sorted. You can have a deep marriage with clear lines. In fact you can't have one without them.

A boundary isn't a punishment. "I'm setting a boundary" is not a sentence you say to hurt someone. If it's aimed at them, it's a weapon, not a line.

A boundary isn't an ultimatum. An ultimatum tries to control their behavior: *do this or else*. A boundary controls yours: *if that happens, this is what I'll do*. The difference is the whole thing.

A boundary isn't selfish. Selfishness takes what isn't yours. A boundary protects what is.

### Two directions

Lines get crossed both ways. Other people cross into your field: taking your time without asking, telling you what to feel, holding you responsible for their moods. You know that one.

The other direction is the one men miss. You cross into theirs. You fix what they didn't ask you to fix. You carry a consequence that was theirs. You manage a feeling that belongs to them. It's almost always dressed as help. "I'm just trying to." But uninvited entry into another man's field is trespass, even with a casserole.

## Part V — The Protocol
### Five moves. In order.

Here's the system. Five moves, in sequence, each one standing on the last. Don't skip ahead to the conversation. The conversation is move five. Most men start there and wonder why it goes badly.

### Move 1 — Clarify what's yours

You can't protect what you can't name. And if you've been fawning for thirty years, you may genuinely not know what you think, want, or feel. That's not a character flaw. It's atrophy.

Start by asking, several times a day, four questions:

1. What am I feeling right now? One word.
2. What do I actually want in this situation?
3. What matters to me here, not what matters to them?
4. What, in this, is actually mine to handle?

If the answers won't come, start smaller. What do you want for dinner. Which chair. Which route home. Practice having preferences out loud. It sounds absurd, and it's how the muscle grows back.

### Move 2 — Release what isn't

Now the other side of the line. Go back to the weight inventory from Part I and look at everything you marked *theirs*. For each one, say the release statement out loud. Yes, out loud. Yes, alone in the truck.

- Their feelings are theirs to manage.
- Their choices are theirs to make.
- Their consequences are theirs to carry.
- Their happiness is theirs to build.
- I cannot control this. Only they can.

Release isn't indifference. You still care. You're just no longer pretending that your care gives you the authority, or the obligation, to run their life. Write the three hardest ones here:

I release ______________________________________________
I release ______________________________________________
I release ______________________________________________

### Move 3 — Draw the line

A limit that isn't specific isn't a limit. It's a mood. Every real boundary has five parts:

1. **What.** The specific behavior or situation.
2. **Who.** The person it applies to.
3. **When.** Under what circumstances.
4. **How.** What you will or won't do.
5. **Then.** What happens if it's crossed. Not what they'll do. What you'll do.

Three examples, so you can hear the shape:

> I don't take work calls after seven. If someone calls, it goes to voicemail and I reply the next business day.

> When Mom criticizes how we're raising the kids, I'll say "I'm not open to feedback on this" and change the subject. If she keeps going, I'll end the visit.

> I'm not lending my brother money again. If he asks, I'll say no and offer to sit down with him and his budget.

Notice what's missing. No explanation of why. No request for their agreement. No threat. Just what you'll do.

Write your three. The ones that have been sitting in your chest since Part I.

Limit 1 ___________________________________________________________
Limit 2 ___________________________________________________________
Limit 3 ___________________________________________________________

Unwritten limits get renegotiated under pressure. Written ones hold.

### Move 4 — Let reality teach

A boundary with no consequence is a suggestion. And the consequence is almost never something you do *to* them. It's something you stop doing *for* them.

If you yell, I'll leave the room. If you don't do your part, the project fails and that's yours. If you keep drinking, I won't call your boss for you. If this continues, I'll see you less.

The rescue is the thing that's been breaking everything. Every time you step between a man and the result of his choices, you teach him that results don't apply to him. You remove the one thing that might have changed him. You are, with the best intentions, keeping him exactly where he is.

Letting it land means watching someone you love struggle and not moving. It is the hardest thing in this book. You will feel like a monster. You aren't. You didn't make the consequence. His choices did. You're just no longer standing in front of it.

### Move 5 — Hold it, kindly

When you draw a new line, pushback is guaranteed. The people who benefited from your lack of boundaries will fight to get the old you back. Expect all of it:

- Guilt. "After everything I've done for you."
- Anger. "You're being selfish."
- Threats. "If you do this, I'll."
- Victim. "I can't believe you'd treat me this way."
- Bargaining. "Just this once. I'll change."
- Denial. "You don't mean that."
- Recruitment. Getting your mother, your wife, your pastor to lean on you.

None of it is evidence that you're wrong. It's evidence that something is changing. Here's the hold, four lines, in order:

1. Name their feeling. "I understand this is hard for you."
2. Restate the line. "And this is what I'm able to do."
3. Decline the debate. "I'm not going to argue about it. My answer stands."
4. Leave if you need to. "I'm going to end this conversation now."

Don't explain more than once. Explanation invites negotiation. Don't match their heat. Warm voice, straight spine. And don't apologize for the line. You can be sorry that it's hard for them. You are not sorry that you drew it.

Clarify. Release. Limit. Allow. Hold.

That's the whole protocol. It's not complicated. It's just expensive, and you pay in reps.

## Part VI — The Words
### What to say when it's time

The hardest part is usually the sentence. Men stall for months not because they don't know what the boundary is but because they can't find words that aren't either apologetic or cruel. So here are words. Say them in your own voice, but say them.

### Saying no

> No, that doesn't work for me.
> I'm not able to do that.
> I understand this puts you in a spot, and the answer is still no.
> I've given you my answer. I'm not going to explain further.

No is a complete sentence. Every word you add after it is a handle for them to pull.

### Protecting your time

> I have a commitment then. (The commitment can be to your family, or to yourself. You don't have to say which.)
> I'm not available then. I could do Thursday.
> I don't take calls after seven. I'll get back to you tomorrow.
> Let me check and get back to you.

That last one is the most useful sentence in this book. It buys you the time to decide without the pressure of their face.

### Unwanted advice

> I appreciate that you care. I'm not looking for input on this.
> I've made my decision.
> If I want advice, I'll ask for it.

### Criticism

> You're entitled to your opinion. I see it differently.
> I hear that you're unhappy with it. I'm at peace with it.
> You don't have to agree with me.

### Refusing to carry their feelings

> Your feelings make sense. I can't fix them for you.
> I'm sorry you're upset. What do you need to do to work through it?
> I didn't make you feel that. You're having a reaction, and it's yours.
> I can be here with you. I can't take it away.

### Letting a consequence land

> I'm not going to rescue you from this. I think you can handle it.
> This is the result of your choice. I trust you'll figure it out.
> I love you too much to keep you from learning this.

### Guilt trips

> I understand you're disappointed. My decision stands.
> I can care about your feelings and still say no.
> I'm not responsible for how you feel about my boundary.

### Ending it

> I've said what I'm going to say.
> This isn't productive. I'm going to stop here.
> I'm going to hang up now. We can talk when things are calmer.

### Announcing a new line

When you're changing the rules in a relationship that's run the old way for years, don't spring it. Say it plainly, once:

1. What you've noticed. "I've realized I've been taking on things that aren't mine."
2. What's changing. "Going forward, I'm going to."
3. Why, if you want to. "This is what I need to be healthy in this relationship."
4. Room for their reaction, without budging. "I know this is different. My decision's made."

Say what you mean. Mean what you say. Don't say it mean.

## Part VII — Holding It
### The daily audit and the long game

Drawing a line is a moment. Holding it is a life. The lines you set this month will drift by Christmas unless you check them, and the people who want the old you back are patient.

### The daily audit

Five questions. At night, with a pen, two minutes:

1. What did I take responsibility for today that wasn't mine?
2. Where did I say yes to avoid guilt or a fight?
3. What consequence am I keeping from reaching someone?
4. What line do I need to restate tomorrow?
5. Where did I hold? Where did I fold?

The last one matters most. Men only log the failures. Log the holds. They're the proof the muscle is growing.

### The resentment check

Any time resentment shows up, treat it as the alarm it is and go find the fire.

- What line got crossed?
- Did they cross it, or did I?
- What do I need that I'm not getting?
- What limit do I need to set, or set again?

Don't silence the alarm. Don't pour a drink on it. Read it.

### When you fold

You will. Here's what to do when you do:

1. Name it without shame. "I folded. That's information, not identity."
2. Figure out what got you. Fear? Guilt? Habit? Tired?
3. Restate the line. To them, if it needs saying.
4. Write the next one. "When X happens, I'll do Y."
5. Get back to work. One fold is a rep. Quitting is the only real failure.

Progress isn't perfection. It's folding less often, catching it faster, and getting up quicker.

### The hard rooms

Some relationships will test every line you draw.

**Your family of origin.** This is where you learned it, and they'll fight hardest to keep it. Expect that. The guilt will be the most refined guilt you'll ever face. Stay the course. You can love your mother and not be managed by her.

**Your wife.** Marriage isn't the abolition of the line. You're one flesh and you're still two people. Some of the worst boundary collapse I've seen is between husbands and wives who thought love meant having no self left. Learn to love her with a self intact. She'll have more to love.

**Your kids.** The line moves as they grow. The whole job is slowly handing them their own pack, one piece at a time, until they can carry it. A father who carries it for them at eighteen isn't loving them. He's keeping them small.

**Work.** Define what you will and won't do, when you're reachable, and what's outside your job. Nobody will do it for you, and the ones who benefit from your lack of a line will never bring it up.

**The one who won't respect any line.** Some people will cross every boundary you draw, every time. With them, the line may be distance. Less contact. Sometimes none. That's not cruelty. For some relationships it's the only loving thing left, for both of you.

### The line you draw with yourself

Boundaries aren't only about other people. The man who can't say no to himself will never hold a line with anyone else.

- A limit on acting the moment you want to. Eating, spending, speaking, reacting.
- A limit on how long you'll stay in a spiral. Rumination has a clock, and you set it.
- A limit on how long you'll sit in a dark mood before you move your body.
- A limit that protects what you've committed to against what you feel like at the moment.

Self-governance is the foundation under all the rest. Aurelius again: *The first rule is to keep an untroubled spirit. The second is to look things in the face and know them for what they are.* That's internal boundary work, in two sentences, from an emperor.

### What it looks like when it holds

You'll know it's working not because the conflict is gone but because the resentment is. Give it ninety days and watch for this:

- The low anger toward the people you help fades, because you're no longer giving past the line.
- Relationships get cleaner. Everyone knows whose job is whose.
- Respect goes up. From them. From you.
- Energy comes back. You weren't tired from the work. You were tired from the weight.
- Love becomes something you choose instead of something you owe.
- You know where you end. And it turns out there's a man there.

This isn't selfishness. It's stewardship. Proper care of what's yours. Proper release of what isn't.

### The minimum

If you do nothing else from this book, do this. Two questions, every night, out loud or on paper:

**What did I carry today that wasn't mine?**
**What will I put down tomorrow?**

That's it. That's the whole protocol in two sentences. The rest of this book is just helping you answer them honestly.
