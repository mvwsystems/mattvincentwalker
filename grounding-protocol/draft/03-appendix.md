## Appendix A — The inventory
### Twenty statements, four grounds

A longer read on the four grounds that catch most men. Rate each from 1 (never) to 5 (always). Total each section. Your highest section is your primary ground — and where to aim the Protocol first.

**A. Performance**
- I feel anxious when I'm not being productive.
- My sense of worth goes up when I accomplish something significant.
- Rest feels like wasted time unless I've earned it.
- I compare my output to other men's regularly.
- A failed project affects how I see myself for days or longer.
Section total: ___ / 25

**B. Approval**
- I change how I act significantly depending on who I'm with.
- Criticism affects my mood out of proportion.
- I replay conversations looking for signs someone's disappointed in me.
- I have trouble saying no even when I should.
- I need positive feedback to feel confident about my work.
Section total: ___ / 25

**C. Control**
- Uncertainty makes me significantly anxious.
- I have trouble delegating important things.
- I plan heavily to reduce the chance of being surprised.
- When things don't go to plan, I get noticeably distressed.
- I feel safer with more information, even when I can't act on it.
Section total: ___ / 25

**D. Competence**
- I avoid situations where I might look like I don't know what I'm doing.
- Being wrong in public feels catastrophic.
- I over-prepare so I never look unprepared.
- I feel threatened when someone knows more than I do in my area.
- I have trouble admitting I don't know something.
Section total: ___ / 25

**Reading it:** 5–10, light attachment. 11–17, moderate — a vulnerability under pressure. 18–25, this is likely a primary ground.

## Appendix B — The research behind it
### So you know it isn't just my opinion

Everything in this book I learned the hard way first. But I'm not asking you to take my word for it, and you shouldn't. Here's where the claims come from.

**That your brain treats identity threat like physical threat.** Naomi Eisenberger's work at UCLA showed that social pain — rejection, exclusion, exposure — runs on the same neural circuitry as physical pain. Matthew Lieberman's *Social* lays out the same evidence for a general reader. This is why a critical email can feel like a punch. It is one, as far as your brain is concerned.

**That chronic threat leaves a physical signature.** Bruce McEwen's decades of work on allostatic load describe exactly the cortisol baseline, the hypervigilance, and the sleep disruption in Part II. Stephen Porges's polyvagal theory explains why a nervous system that never stands down can't restore itself. Bessel van der Kolk's *The Body Keeps the Score* is the book most men have heard of; it's about trauma, but the mechanism is the same one running under conditional ground.

**That worth built on outcomes doesn't produce stability.** Jennifer Crocker and Connie Wolfe's research on contingencies of self-worth found that people who stake their self-esteem on external domains show more emotional volatility, more depressive symptoms, and lower well-being — and that *success* in those domains doesn't fix it. It produces a treadmill. That finding is the whole of Part II's loop.

**That shame and guilt are different things.** June Tangney and Ronda Dearing's *Shame and Guilt* established the distinction this book depends on — guilt as "I did something bad," shame as "I am something bad" — and showed that shame, not guilt, drives the hiding, the anger, and the isolation. Brené Brown's work made it plain for a wider audience.

**That men manage mortality by building projects.** Terror management theory — Sheldon Solomon, Jeff Greenberg, and Tom Pyszczynski's *The Worm at the Core* — shows that we handle the fact of death by investing in structures of meaning, and that when those structures are threatened, we get defensive. Marcus Aurelius knew this without the data. So did Viktor Frankl, who found in a concentration camp that men with a reason to live survived what men with only outcomes didn't. Paul Tillich's *The Courage to Be* and Irvin Yalom's *Existential Psychotherapy* work the same ground from philosophy and from the clinic.

**That the ground has to be received, not earned.** This is older than any study. Martin Luther, *The Freedom of a Christian*. Calvin's *Institutes*. Kierkegaard's *The Sickness Unto Death*, which is about exactly the despair of a self that is trying to be its own ground. Bonhoeffer on costly grace. C. S. Lewis, *Mere Christianity*. Tim Keller's *Counterfeit Gods* is the clearest modern treatment of the seven false grounds as functional idols, and if you read one book on this list, read that one.

**The scriptures this book stands on.** Genesis 1:26–27 (made in the image of God). Matthew 3:17 and 4:1–11 (loved before the work; tested on every ground and standing on none). Matthew 7:24–27 (the two houses). Romans 5:1–2 and 8:1, 38–39 (standing secured, nothing can separate). Galatians 3:10–14 (the curse of earning). Ephesians 1:3–14 and 2:8–9 (received, not achieved). Philippians 3:7–11 (Paul moving his weight off achievement). Colossians 2:10 (complete in Him). 1 John 4:18 (perfect love casts out fear).
